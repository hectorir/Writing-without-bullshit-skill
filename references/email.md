# Email Playbook

Loaded when the format is email, message, reply, or "send to." Apply alongside the 10 line-edit habits in `SKILL.md`.

Target length: under 250 words. If you need more, you probably need a different format (memo, doc link, meeting).

## Anatomy of a good email

Six parts, in order. None are optional.

1. **Subject line carries the ask.** Put the action and the deadline in it.
   - ✅ "Need your sign-off on the contract by Thursday"
   - ❌ "Quick question"
   - ❌ "Following up"

2. **One-line greeting.** If the recipient knows you, just their name. If not, one line of context: "I'm a colleague of Alan's in finance."

3. **One-sentence summary.** The whole point, up front. If they read nothing else, this sentence should land. Front-load aggressively.

4. **Facts on one topic.** One email = one topic. Use bullets, **bolded openers**, and links for detail. If you have two topics, send two emails.

5. **Call to action with a deadline.** What you want them to do, and by when. Specific date beats "ASAP."

6. **Brief sign-off.** Name and contact info. Done. No "Have a great day," no "Hope this finds you well," no "Don't hesitate to reach out."

## Etiquette rules

- **Don't compose important emails on your phone.** You can't think and edit well there.
- **Reply, don't reply-all.** Default to the smallest audience that needs the reply.
- **If you have nothing to add, send nothing.** "Got it" and "Thanks!" are noise.
- **Read the latest message in a thread first.** The problem may already be solved.
- **After three back-and-forths, stop typing and call.** Email is failing if it takes four rounds.

## Cold email template

When asking a stranger for help, customize each one. Aim for under 150 words.

Required elements:

1. **Show you know who they are.** One specific reference to their work — a post, a project, a public comment. Generic flattery ("I love your work") backfires.
2. **State your ask plainly.** No five-paragraph windup.
3. **What's in it for them.** Why is this interesting to *them*, not just to you?
4. **One clear next step.** A specific question, a 15-minute call request with two time options, or a yes/no decision.

Avoid:
- Mass-blast templates with merge tags
- Long elaborate self-introductions
- Asks that require the recipient to do significant work before they know if they care

## When you're delivering bad news

Lead with the problem. Take responsibility. List the actions you're taking, ideally as bullets. End with what's next or context that's actually relevant. Cheerful sign-offs after bad news make things worse, not better.
